Sobriety Coaching

How I Help People Build a Sober Life They Actually Want

By Alexis  ·  June 05, 2026  ·  6 min read

Most sobriety coaching sounds like someone reading from a manual written in 1985. I didn't get sober to trade one set of rules for another. What I actually do is help people figure out what a sober life looks like when nobody's watching, when the pressure's off, when it's just them and their own reasons for staying clean.

What Sobriety Coaching Actually Means

A sobriety coach isn't a therapist, a sponsor, or someone who sits across from you with a clipboard. At least not in my version. What I do is look at where someone is right now, where they actually want to be, and help them see the gap between those two places. Then we build the steps. Not the steps in the program, just the actual steps that make sense for their life.

I spent nine years in recovery spaces before I started doing this work. I was a recovery support specialist, an ABA therapist, a case manager, a sober house manager. I've sat in rooms where people pretended to work steps they didn't believe in. I've watched people stay sober out of fear instead of out of genuine choice. That gap between obligatory sobriety and chosen sobriety is where everything falls apart.

What I bring is someone who has actually been through it and didn't take the traditional path. No treatment center. No 12-step sponsor. Just a Tuesday decision at 24 years old and then the unglamorous work of figuring out who I was without the thing that had organized my entire personality. That experience means I don't need someone to lie to me about their recovery. I already know what the lies sound like.

Building Real Accountability as a Sober Accountability Coach

Accountability gets thrown around like it's some magic ingredient. People think it means someone checking in to make sure you didn't relapse. That's surveillance, not accountability. Real accountability is you understanding your own triggers, your own patterns, your own capacity on any given day, and then making decisions from that actual self-knowledge instead of from shame or denial.

When someone works with me, we're not building a system to catch them doing something wrong. We're building a system where they can see themselves clearly. Why did you drink at that wedding? Not because you're weak. Maybe because you hadn't eaten, you were standing alone, you hadn't prepared a conversation starter, and you were in a space where you typically used. That's not a character flaw. That's useful data. Next time, eat before you go. Find one person to talk to beforehand. Have an exit strategy.

I ask hard questions. Not in a judgmental way, but in a direct way. What were you actually feeling when you picked up the bottle? What do you think would have happened if you'd sat with that feeling for five minutes instead? What do you need that you're not getting right now? These aren't rhetorical. I actually want to know. And then we figure out what's possible with the resources you have, not the resources someone thinks you should have.

The Part Nobody Talks About: Building a Life Worth Staying Sober For

Sobriety isn't the goal. A life worth living is the goal. Sobriety is just the thing that makes it possible. I've watched people stay sober for years and still feel completely empty. They white-knuckled through it, they followed all the rules, and they woke up one day realizing they had no idea what they actually wanted. Then they relapsed because sobriety was boring.

What I actually do is help people build lives that feel meaningful. That might mean figuring out work that doesn't make them want to drink. It might mean setting boundaries with family members who activate their worst impulses. It might mean finding community that isn't built around recovery talk. It might mean developing skills that feel impossible right now but become possible with practice and accountability. This is where my background in coaching high-potential people becomes real. I know how to help someone go from survival mode to actually building something.

I'm not interested in keeping people sober through fear or obligation. That falls apart. I'm interested in helping them see that sobriety gives them access to their own thinking, their own capacity, their own ability to make choices that actually align with who they want to be. That's the thing that sticks.

How the Work Actually Happens

This isn't a one-size-fits-all process. Some people need someone to help them think through immediate crisis. Some people are years sober and realizing they built their sobriety on avoidance instead of healing. Some people have never actually committed to recovery and we need to start there. I meet people where they actually are, not where I think they should be.

What typically happens is we start with clarity. I ask a lot of questions and listen to what's beneath the surface. What's driving this person right now? Is it external pressure, internal desire, or some combination? What have they already tried? What worked, even a little? What didn't work at all? From there, we build a plan that's specific to their life, not borrowed from someone else's recovery story. If you need a clarity call to figure out if this is the right fit, that's the place to start.

I'm not a licensed therapist, and I'm clear about that. If someone needs trauma processing or medication management or diagnosis, I refer them to the right people. What I do is coach around behavior, decision-making, accountability, and building a life that feels sustainable. I support all pathways to recovery, non-12-step, harm reduction, motivational interviewing, trauma-informed. The method matters less than the actual commitment to your own life.

Why This Matters Right Now

The recovery industry has a massive problem. It works well for some people and it actively harms others. There are people who find 12-step programs genuinely helpful and there are people who tried them, felt gaslit, and quit recovery entirely because of it. Both of those are real. There are people who need residential treatment and there are people who got sober without it. Both are valid. What's missing is someone saying out loud that recovery doesn't have to look like what you've already tried.

What I do is offer an alternative that's grounded in actual experience, not ideology. I got sober at 24. I cycled through 20 jobs and made it work anyway. I built a freelance income from zero. I learned how to help people think clearly about hard things. I live the life I wanted to build when I was early in recovery and thought it was impossible. That's what I bring to the work.

If you're looking for a sobriety coach or sober accountability coach who treats you like a capable person instead of a problem to fix, that's what I do.
