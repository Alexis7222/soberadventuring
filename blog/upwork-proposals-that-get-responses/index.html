How to Write Upwork Proposals That Get Responses

By Alexis  ·  June 23, 2026  ·  6 min read

Most Upwork proposals get ignored, and the reason is almost never the freelancer's skills. The problem is that proposals are written like cover letters when clients are looking for something much closer to a conversation opener. This guide covers what separates proposals that get responses from the ones that disappear into the queue.

Why Most Upwork Proposals Fail Before the Client Reads Them

Upwork shows clients a preview of your proposal before they open it, which means your first one or two sentences function almost identically to a subject line in a cold email. If those sentences begin with 'I am a highly skilled professional with X years of experience,' the client has likely already scrolled past. The preview text is the only real gate between your proposal and a response, and most freelancers spend zero time on it.

According to Upwork's own data, clients receive an average of 20 to 50 proposals per job posting in competitive categories. In that context, a generic opener is not just weak, it is invisible. The proposals that get opened tend to reference something specific from the job post right away, which signals to the client that the freelancer actually read it rather than copied and pasted a template.

The other factor that often gets overlooked is timing. Upwork ranks proposals partly by submission time, meaning the first 5 to 10 proposals submitted tend to get significantly more visibility. Checking for new listings frequently and responding within an hour of posting can meaningfully improve your open rate before a single word of your proposal does any work.

The Structure of a Proposal That Gets Clients on Upwork to Respond

A proposal that converts tends to follow a loose but consistent shape: open with a specific observation about the client's problem, offer a concrete idea or question that demonstrates you understand the work, and close with a low-friction call to action. The whole thing should be short enough to read in under a minute. Proposals under 150 words often outperform longer ones, because they respect the client's time and signal that you can communicate clearly.

The opening should never be about you. It should reflect the client's situation back to them in a way that shows you read the post carefully. If the job description mentions a specific pain point, a tight deadline, or an outcome they're trying to reach, referencing that directly is one of the fastest ways to make the client feel like they're being heard rather than pitched. This is not flattery; it's just demonstrating relevance.

The middle of the proposal is where you can briefly introduce what you'd do and why you're positioned to do it well. One or two sentences on relevant experience is usually enough. What clients are often looking for at this stage is confidence and specificity, and a short case study reference or concrete result will land harder than a list of credentials. The close should make it easy to say yes to a next step, usually a quick call or a clarifying question, rather than asking them to make a hiring decision immediately.

How to Upwork Proposals Stand Out: The Specific Over the General

The most reliable way to differentiate a proposal is to include something the client did not expect. This might be a quick observation about their website, a gap you noticed in the job description, or a specific question that shows you've already started thinking about their problem. This is sometimes called a 'value add' in sales, and it works because it shifts the dynamic from 'I want this job' to 'I've already started working on your problem.'

When Alexis first started on Upwork, the contracts that converted fastest were the ones where she led with a specific insight rather than a pitch. Cold calling experience made it second nature to open with the prospect's situation before ever mentioning her own. That same principle applies directly to Upwork proposals: you are not selling yourself, you are showing the client you understand what they need.

Avoid generic phrases like 'I am very interested in this opportunity' or 'I believe I would be a great fit.' These phrases carry no information and take up space that could hold something useful. Specificity is not just a stylistic preference here; it is the functional difference between a proposal that sounds like everyone else's and one that reads like it came from someone who already understands the job.

Pricing, Rates, and What to Say When Clients Ask

One of the most common mistakes new freelancers make is underbidding in hopes of standing out on price. On Upwork, very low rates often raise red flags rather than attracting interest, because clients have been burned by cheap proposals before and have learned to associate them with unreliable outcomes. A rate that reflects real market value, even if it's higher than you're comfortable with at first, tends to attract more serious clients.

If you're unsure what to charge, look at the rates visible on comparable profiles in your category. Upwork shows hourly rates publicly, and spending 20 minutes researching what experienced freelancers in your niche charge will give you a realistic range to work from. For project-based work, it's often worth asking a clarifying question before quoting, because scoping a project without understanding its full requirements can lead to undercharging or overshooting what the client actually needs.

When a client asks about rate before the conversation has gone anywhere, a clean response is to briefly acknowledge the question and offer to discuss it after you understand the scope better. This is not evasion; it's a reasonable professional norm in any service business, and most serious clients will respect it.

Your Profile Does Half the Work Before the Proposal Arrives

A strong proposal can still lose to a weak profile. When a client opens your proposal, their next move is almost always to click through to your profile, and what they find there either reinforces or undercuts the impression you made. Your profile headline and overview need to speak directly to the type of client you want, using the same language they'd use to describe their own problem, not the language you'd use to describe your skill set.

Social proof matters significantly on Upwork, and the platform's review system means that early jobs carry disproportionate weight. If you're just starting out and have no reviews yet, it's worth considering a short, lower-stakes project just to build your feedback history, even if the rate is not where you ultimately want it to be. One or two strong reviews can change how your profile reads entirely.

Your portfolio should show work that is as close as possible to what you want to be hired to do. Clients often make fast judgments based on the first sample they see, so ordering your portfolio with the most relevant work first, rather than the work you're most proud of in general, will serve you better in practice.

Getting clients on Upwork is largely a skill of communication before it's a skill of anything else. A focused proposal, a specific opener, and a profile that says clearly what you do and who you do it for will get you further than any template or shortcut.
