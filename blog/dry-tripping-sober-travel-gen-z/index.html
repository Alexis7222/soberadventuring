Dry Tripping: What Gen Z Wants From Sober Travel

By Alexis  ·  June 11, 2026  ·  6 min read

The dry tripping sober travel trend has moved well past niche status. A growing share of Gen Z travelers are deliberately choosing trips built around clear-headed experiences, and the data and the hospitality industry are starting to catch up. Whether you're fully sober, sober-curious, or simply tired of waking up hungover in a city you paid a lot of money to see, this article breaks down what's driving the shift and what it looks like in practice.

What Dry Tripping Means

Dry tripping refers to traveling without alcohol, intentionally and as a feature of the trip rather than an afterthought. The term has circulated on TikTok and in travel media since roughly 2022, picking up momentum alongside broader conversations about sober curiosity and mental health among younger adults. It covers a wide spectrum: people in long-term recovery, people taking a deliberate break from drinking, and people who simply want to see what travel feels like without defaulting to bar culture.

Sober curiosity, a term popularized by Ruby Warrington's 2018 book of the same name, sits at the cultural center of this shift. It describes a deliberate, questioning relationship with alcohol rather than a binary abstinence-or-not framework. For many Gen Z travelers, dry tripping is one expression of that curiosity applied to a specific context. The trip becomes an experiment: what do I want to do when drinking isn't the organizing principle of each evening?

It's worth separating dry tripping from recovery travel, though there's significant overlap. Someone in recovery may plan a trip specifically around sobriety-friendly infrastructure, meetings, or wellness-focused itineraries. A dry tripper might simply be choosing not to drink for the week without any broader recovery context. Both approaches are valid, and the distinction matters mainly because the planning needs differ.

The Data Behind the Dry Tripping Sober Travel Trend

Gen Z drinks less than any previous generation at the same age, and that behavioral shift is now showing up in how they travel. A 2023 Berenberg Research report found that Gen Z consumes roughly 20% less alcohol than Millennials did at the same age. Separately, a NielsenIQ report noted that the no- and low-alcohol beverage category grew by over 7% globally in 2022, driven disproportionately by younger consumers. These aren't marginal trends.

The hospitality industry has noticed. Sober bars have opened in cities including London, New York, and Los Angeles, and a growing number of hotels and resorts now market specifically to wellness-oriented travelers who want curated non-alcoholic experiences. Virtuoso, a luxury travel network, reported in 2023 that wellness travel bookings had increased by over 30% year-over-year, with many clients explicitly requesting alcohol-free itinerary options. Airlines including Delta and Finnair have expanded their non-alcoholic cocktail menus in response to demand.

On social media, the hashtag #sobertravel has accumulated hundreds of millions of views across platforms, with content ranging from sober backpacking vlogs to alcohol-free cruise reviews. What's notable is the tone: most of this content is not framed around struggle or deprivation. It's framed around quality, around what becomes possible when the trip isn't structured around drinking.

Why Gen Z Is Choosing This

Several overlapping factors seem to be driving the trend. Mental health awareness is higher among Gen Z than prior generations, and many younger adults have made explicit connections between their alcohol use and their anxiety, sleep quality, and mood. Travel is already a disruption to routine, which can amplify those effects. Skipping alcohol on a trip may feel lower-stakes as a first experiment precisely because the context is already different from everyday life.

Cost is also a genuine factor. International travel is expensive, and alcohol can represent 20-30% of a travel budget in destinations where going out is central to the tourist experience. A traveler who opts out of that spending pattern can redirect funds toward experiences, accommodation, or simply staying longer. For a generation that came of age during economic instability, that calculus is not abstract.

There's also something worth noting about memory and presence. Travel photographs and documents experiences, and some travelers may find that they want full recall of what they saw and did. This isn't a moral claim about drinking; it's a practical one about what kind of travel a person wants to have. For those who have had blackout experiences while traveling, or who have lost days of a trip to hangovers, the appeal of clarity might be straightforward.

How to Plan a Dry Trip

The most useful starting point is destination research that goes beyond the obvious. Some cities are structurally easier to navigate sober than others, partly because of culture and partly because of infrastructure. Destinations with strong coffee culture, active nightlife alternatives, late-night food scenes, or wellness-oriented tourism tend to be more accessible. Chiang Mai, for instance, has a well-developed community of digital nomads and wellness travelers, active night markets, and a food culture that doesn't center on alcohol. Lisbon has a thriving specialty coffee scene alongside its wine reputation. The point is that almost any destination works; the planning question is what you'll do with your evenings.

Accommodation choice matters more than most dry trippers anticipate. Booking a hotel with a strong lobby or communal space, a hostel with organized activities, or a wellness resort with programming can significantly reduce the feeling of being socially adrift in the evenings. The moment that most often trips people up on a dry trip is the post-dinner window when the default move would have been to find a bar. Having an intentional answer to that window is the single most practical piece of planning you can do.

If you're in recovery and traveling internationally, it's worth researching meeting availability at your destination before you leave. Alcoholics Anonymous has meetings in over 180 countries, and the AA international directory is searchable by city. SMART Recovery also has an online meeting schedule that works across time zones. Neither of these requires a commitment to a particular recovery identity; they're simply resources that exist if you want a community anchor while you're away from your home support structure.

What Sober Travel Might Feel Like

For people in early recovery, travel can feel riskier than staying home, and that concern is legitimate. New environments disrupt routines, and routine is often what keeps a recovery practice stable. If you're in the first year of sobriety, a trip that involves significant social pressure to drink, like a bachelor party or a festival with limited alcohol-free options, might be worth declining or postponing. That's not a permanent limitation; it's a timing consideration.

For people who are more established in their sobriety or who are simply experimenting with drinking less, dry tripping often surfaces a version of travel that feels more detailed. You may notice more: the light in a place, the texture of a conversation, the specific flavor of a meal. Some travelers report that the absence of a hangover half-day genuinely expands what they can do. Others find that the first few evenings feel awkward and that the discomfort fades. Neither outcome is guaranteed, and individual experience varies considerably.

One thing that comes up repeatedly in sober travel communities is the social dimension. Alcohol is a social lubricant in many cultures, and opting out can occasionally create friction, particularly in group travel contexts. Having a ready, low-drama response to drink offers, something like 'I'm not drinking this trip, but I'll take a sparkling water,' tends to work better than lengthy explanations. Most people, in most situations, are less focused on your drinking choices than it might feel in the moment.

Dry tripping is not a sacrifice framework; it's a deliberate travel choice that a growing number of people are finding genuinely better fits what they want from time away. The planning is real, the adjustment may take a day or two, and the payoff in presence and recall can be significant.
