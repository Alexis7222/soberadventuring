SMART Recovery vs. AA: An Honest Comparison

By Alexis  ·  June 17, 2026  ·  6 min read

Choosing between SMART Recovery and AA is one of the first real decisions people face when getting sober, and it often gets made based on whoever spoke louder at the treatment center. This article covers how each program works in practice, where the research stands, and what tends to determine which approach fits a given person.

What SMART Recovery Is

SMART stands for Self-Management and Recovery Training. It is a nonprofit organization founded in 1994 that offers free mutual-aid meetings, both in person and online, built around cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and rational emotive behavior therapy (REBT). Unlike AA, SMART does not use a higher power framework, does not assign sponsors, and does not require any belief in powerlessness over addiction. The program treats addiction as a complex behavior that can be changed through specific skills, not as a disease requiring lifelong surrender.

The SMART model is organized around a four-point program: building motivation to change, coping with urges, managing thoughts and behaviors that drive use, and building a life that makes sobriety sustainable. Each meeting tends to be more structured than a typical AA meeting, often led by a trained facilitator who guides skill practice rather than open sharing. The approach is explicitly secular, though SMART does not require members to be atheists or agnostics — it simply does not make spirituality a condition of participation.

One practical advantage of SMART is that it welcomes people dealing with any addictive behavior, whether substance-related or behavioral, which means someone with overlapping issues may find one meeting more relevant than having to sort through multiple programs. The organization also makes its workbooks and tools publicly available, so members can use them between meetings without needing to track down a sponsor or complete formal step work.

How AA Works and What the Research Says

Alcoholics Anonymous was founded in 1935 and is by far the most widely available peer recovery program in the world, with an estimated 2 million members across more than 180 countries. Its model is built on the 12 steps, which include admitting powerlessness over alcohol, working through moral inventories, making amends, and maintaining a spiritual practice. AA is explicitly not a religious organization, though the language of a "higher power" runs throughout its literature, and many meetings have a distinctly spiritual tone.

For a long time, AA's effectiveness was difficult to measure rigorously because participation is anonymous and self-selected. That changed significantly with a 2020 Cochrane Review — one of the most thorough systematic reviews in addiction research — which analyzed 27 studies involving over 10,000 participants. The review found that AA and 12-step facilitation programs were more effective than other treatments at producing continuous sobriety, and were at least as effective as CBT-based approaches at reducing drinking frequency and severity. The finding that surprised many clinicians was how strong the community effect appeared to be: regular meeting attendance and having a sponsor were both associated with better outcomes.

The research on AA is stronger than its critics often acknowledge, and weaker than its proponents sometimes claim. Long-term sobriety rates in AA are hard to calculate accurately because of dropout, but Stanford psychiatrist Keith Humphreys, one of the lead researchers on the Cochrane Review, has noted in subsequent interviews that the community infrastructure of AA — daily meetings, phone contact, sponsorship — produces a level of support that most clinical programs simply cannot replicate. That does not make AA the right fit for everyone, but it does mean dismissing it as unscientific misses the point of what it provides.

Where SMART Recovery Research Stands

The evidence base for SMART Recovery is smaller than for AA, largely because the organization is younger and has fewer resources for large-scale studies. What does exist is generally positive. A 2014 study published in the journal Addictive Behaviors found that SMART participants showed significant reductions in depression, anxiety, and substance use over a 12-month follow-up period. A separate Australian study tracking over 500 SMART participants found that about 60 percent reported abstinence or significant reduction in use at a one-year follow-up, with higher meeting attendance correlating with better outcomes.

The CBT tools SMART uses — particularly urge surfing, cost-benefit analysis of using, and disputing irrational beliefs — are well-supported in clinical research independent of SMART itself. That matters because it means a person can use SMART's framework to build skills that are grounded in decades of psychological research, not just program tradition. The weaker point is that SMART has far fewer meetings available than AA in most cities, and its online meeting infrastructure, while functional, does not yet replicate the density of in-person AA communities in most regions.

One honest limitation worth naming: most SMART research relies on self-report and lacks long-term follow-up beyond two years. That is a common limitation across addiction research in general, but it means confident claims about SMART's long-term effectiveness compared to AA are premature. The honest answer is that the research supports SMART's short-to-medium term effectiveness, and the comparison to AA over five or ten years is largely unanswered.

What Determines Which Program Fits

The most consistent finding across addiction research is that engagement and consistency matter more than which specific program someone chooses. A person who genuinely connects with AA and attends regularly will almost always do better than someone who dabbles in SMART without applying the tools. That said, fit is real, and forcing someone into a program they find alienating or intellectually unconvincing tends to produce early dropout rather than recovery.

People who often gravitate toward SMART tend to have a preference for cognitive frameworks over narrative-based sharing, a skepticism toward spiritual language that makes AA meetings feel uncomfortable rather than neutral, or a desire to set their own goals rather than commit to lifetime abstinence from the outset. People who often do well in AA tend to benefit from its density of community, the structured accountability of step work and sponsorship, and the explicit meaning-making that comes from a spiritual framework, even a loosely defined one.

The false choice is assuming you have to pick one and stay there. I've personally used both, along with Refuge Recovery, and found that what I needed shifted over time. The 12 steps gave me a structure early on when I needed external accountability. SMART's tools for disputing distorted thinking became more useful once I was stable and trying to understand my own patterns. Treating recovery modalities the way you'd treat a financial portfolio — spreading across what works rather than concentrating in one place — is a framework I come back to often. Neither program is a complete answer on its own, and neither deserves to be dismissed.

Practical Considerations for Getting Started

If access is a limiting factor, AA has a significant practical advantage. In most mid-sized cities, daily AA meetings are available within a few miles, and the AA meeting locator covers most English-speaking countries. SMART's in-person meeting coverage is growing but remains uneven, concentrated in larger urban areas. However, SMART's online meeting schedule is extensive — the organization runs dozens of virtual meetings weekly across different time zones, which makes it genuinely accessible for people in rural areas or those with schedule constraints.

Cost is not meaningfully different between the programs. Both are free to attend. SMART sells workbooks and materials, but its core tools are available through its website at no cost. AA's Big Book is available for free online, and most groups ask only for a small voluntary contribution at meetings. Neither program requires financial commitment to participate meaningfully.

If you are currently in a treatment program, most clinical staff can provide referrals to both. It is worth asking specifically for an introduction to SMART if the default recommendation is AA, since many treatment centers still default to 12-step facilitation without presenting alternatives. Some people find it useful to try both in the first 90 days rather than committing early, since the early recovery period often looks different from what sustains sobriety over years.

Neither SMART Recovery nor AA is universally better, and the research does not support treating this as a clean contest. What matters is finding something you will use consistently, and staying honest about whether it is working.
