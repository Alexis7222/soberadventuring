Can You Get Sober Without AA? Yes—Here's How

By Alexis  ·  June 14, 2026  ·  6 min read

AA works for a lot of people, but it doesn't work for everyone, and the research is clear that it isn't the only path to lasting sobriety. If the 12-step model doesn't fit your beliefs, your schedule, or your past experience, there are well-studied alternatives that may serve you better. This article covers the most credible options for sobriety without AA, what the evidence says about each, and how to figure out where to start.

What the Research Says About AA and Sobriety Without AA

AA is the most studied peer support program in addiction recovery, and the evidence in its favor is real. A 2020 Cochrane Review, one of the most rigorous systematic reviews conducted on the subject, found that AA was more effective than other interventions at producing continuous abstinence. About 27% of AA participants maintained sobriety at follow-up, compared to roughly 11% in other groups. That is a meaningful difference, and it deserves to be stated plainly.

What that same research also makes clear, however, is that the majority of people who try AA do not maintain long-term abstinence through it alone, and a significant share stop attending entirely. The dropout rate from 12-step programs is consistently high across studies, which means that for a large portion of people who seek recovery, AA as a standalone solution may not hold. Effectiveness in aggregate statistics doesn't mean effectiveness for any specific individual.

The practical takeaway is that no single program has a monopoly on recovery outcomes. People get sober through medication-assisted treatment, cognitive-behavioral approaches, peer support alternatives, and structured outpatient programs, often in combination. The question isn't whether AA works in general — it's whether it's the right fit for you specifically.

SMART Recovery: The Evidence-Based Secular Alternative

SMART Recovery (Self-Management and Recovery Training) is the most rigorously developed secular alternative to AA currently available. It draws on cognitive-behavioral therapy and motivational interviewing, both of which have strong clinical support in addiction treatment. Rather than framing addiction around powerlessness and a higher power, SMART centers on self-efficacy — the idea that people can build real skills to manage cravings, examine their motivations, and change their behavior over time.

SMART Recovery offers free in-person and online meetings, a structured workbook, and a four-point program covering motivation, coping with urges, problem-solving, and lifestyle balance. Research on SMART is still younger and thinner than the body of evidence behind AA, but early studies show comparable outcomes for participants who engage consistently, and the approach tends to resonate strongly with people who are skeptical of spiritual frameworks or who have found that AA's community culture isn't a fit for them.

One practical advantage of SMART is the online meeting infrastructure, which matters for people in rural areas or countries where in-person options are limited. If you want to explore it, the SMART Recovery website has a meeting finder and free resources that don't require any registration to access.

Refuge Recovery and Other Mindfulness-Based Programs

Refuge Recovery is a peer support program rooted in Buddhist principles, specifically the idea that addiction is a form of craving and that mindfulness practice can interrupt the cycle. It doesn't require belief in Buddhism as a religion, but it does use meditation, ethical reflection, and community as its core tools. The program has a free meeting structure similar to AA and a companion book by Noah Levine that outlines the approach.

Mindfulness-based interventions more broadly have a growing evidence base in addiction research. A 2014 randomized controlled trial published in JAMA Psychiatry found that Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention performed comparably to cognitive-behavioral relapse prevention and significantly better than treatment as usual at reducing substance use over a 12-month follow-up. Mindfulness-based approaches may be especially worth considering for people whose substance use is heavily tied to anxiety or emotional regulation difficulties.

Alexis has direct experience with Refuge Recovery alongside the 12 steps, and the honest assessment is that different frameworks serve different needs at different moments. For some people the sitting practice and philosophical structure of Refuge Recovery fills a gap that AA meetings don't, particularly around understanding the internal experience of craving rather than just managing it behaviorally.

Medication-Assisted Treatment: The Most Underused Option

Medication-assisted treatment, commonly called MAT, combines FDA-approved medications with counseling and behavioral support. For alcohol use disorder specifically, naltrexone, acamprosate, and disulfiram are all FDA-approved and have substantial clinical evidence behind them. Naltrexone in particular has been shown in multiple trials to reduce heavy drinking days and support abstinence, and it can be prescribed by a primary care physician, not just an addiction specialist.

MAT is not a sign of incomplete recovery. This is worth stating clearly because stigma around medication in recovery spaces, including some AA communities, may lead people to avoid it even when it would genuinely help. The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) is unambiguous that MAT is an evidence-based standard of care, not a shortcut. For opioid use disorder, buprenorphine and methadone have among the strongest evidence of any intervention in all of addiction medicine, reducing overdose mortality significantly in multiple large-scale studies.

If cost or access is a barrier, SAMHSA's National Helpline (1-800-662-4357) is free, confidential, and can connect callers to local treatment options including sliding-scale MAT providers. Many people pursue MAT alongside peer support programs rather than as a replacement, which may produce better outcomes than either alone.

Building a Recovery Structure That Doesn't Depend on One Thing

One of the risks of relying entirely on any single program, including AA, is that it creates a single point of failure. If meetings stop being available, if a sponsor relationship breaks down, or if the community stops feeling supportive, the whole structure can become unstable. Diversifying recovery support the way you'd diversify anything important reduces the risk that one disruption brings everything down. This isn't a criticism of AA or any other program — it's just a structural reality.

Practically, this might mean combining a peer support program with individual therapy, or pairing MAT with an online meeting community, or using a workbook-based approach while maintaining a connection to a counselor or case manager. Research on recovery capital, the internal and external resources a person draws on to sustain sobriety, consistently shows that broader, more varied support networks tend to produce more durable outcomes than narrower ones.

The other piece worth naming is that professional support matters. Peer programs are not a substitute for clinical care, especially in early recovery or for people managing co-occurring mental health conditions. A licensed counselor, psychiatrist, or addiction medicine physician can assess what level of care is actually appropriate for someone's situation, which a meeting or a workbook cannot do on its own.

Sobriety without AA is not a compromise — for many people it may be a more honest match for their actual needs. The options covered here are supported by real evidence and used by real people who have built lasting recovery outside the 12-step framework, often alongside other forms of support rather than as a replacement for everything.
