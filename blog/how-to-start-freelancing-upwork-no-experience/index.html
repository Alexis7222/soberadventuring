How to Start Freelancing on Upwork With No Experience

By Alexis  ·  June 5, 2026  ·  7 min read

I had zero freelance experience and a resume full of red flags: more than 20 jobs, gaps I could not explain, fired from some and quit from others. I also had four years working in rehab and mental health spaces, which gave me a specific set of skills that turned out to be more marketable than I expected. I made $30k in my first four months on Upwork by getting specific about what I could actually do and finding clients who needed exactly that.

The Skill You Already Have Is Enough to Start Freelancing on Upwork

Every job that ended badly still gave you something. I listed out everything I had actually done across all those positions: case management, client intake, outreach, motivational interviewing, documentation, de-escalation. None of it had a freelance label on it, but when I looked at what someone would pay for, the pattern was obvious. I understood people, I could run outreach, and I could manage a client relationship without things falling apart.

Look at your work history without the shame attached to it. What would someone pay for that you have already been doing for an employer? That becomes your service. It does not need a polished name, it just needs to be specific enough that a client can picture exactly what you would do for them.

Upwork clients are not hiring resumes. They are hiring someone who can solve a defined problem, and that is a different standard than the traditional job market, one that most people with real-world experience can actually meet.

Build Your Profile Like Someone Will Actually Read It

Your photo should look like a real person, not a badge photo or a decade-old LinkedIn headshot. Your headline should be specific: "Sales Outreach Support for B2B Founders" beats "Virtual Assistant" every single time. Specific language filters out the wrong clients and pulls in the right ones, while generic language disappears in the search results.

Your bio should sound like you actually wrote it. Most Upwork profiles read like someone filled in a cover letter template halfway through and gave up. Write in first person, say what you have done, and if you are new to freelancing, say that. Clients do not hate new, they hate people who misrepresent what they can deliver. Honesty is a real competitive advantage on a platform full of inflated claims.

Take the relevant skill tests for your service area. They are not proof of competence, but they signal that you put in effort, and when you have no reviews yet, those signals carry actual weight.

Price to Win the First Few Projects, Then Raise Your Rates

With no reviews, you cannot compete at market rate. You are buying proof of concept. I charged well below what I eventually charged for the same work just to build up my first five-star reviews, and that was not devaluing myself, it was understanding how a marketplace built on social proof actually functions.

Start with fixed-price projects in the $150 to $400 range, scoped to a clear deliverable. Open-ended hourly work when you are just starting means both of you are guessing at expectations, and that usually ends with a frustrated client and no review. Small and specific gives you the best shot at delivering well, and delivering well is what earns the review.

Three to five solid reviews and you raise your rate. The Upwork algorithm rewards high job success scores and completed contracts, so the early below-market work is buying you a position in the search results over time, not just a handful of stars.

Landing the First Project Takes More Applications Than Most People Expect

Apply to more jobs than feels reasonable. I applied to dozens before landing anything, and most people quit after five or ten rejections and decide the platform does not work. It does work. You just have to get through the early numbers before they start working in your favor.

Write proposals that open with the client's actual problem, not your background. Most proposals start with "Hi, I'm [name] and I have X years of experience..." and nobody reads past the first line. Lead with what you see in their job post and what you would do about it, keep it short, and attach the one piece of work that is closest to relevant proof.

If you do not have a portfolio piece that fits, make one. Write a sample, build a mock-up, record a short video walkthrough. Clients want to see how you think, not a list of past employers.

Once You Have the Project, Make the Review Easy to Give

The fastest way to lose a good client is to go quiet after you land the work. Send updates before they have to ask for them, get all your clarifying questions answered before you start rather than stopping halfway through to figure out what they actually wanted, and deliver on or before the deadline. When the project wraps, ask for a review directly. Something like "I would really appreciate your feedback if you have a moment" is enough, and most clients who had a good experience will respond if you give them the opening.

Feedback, including the critical kind, is how you move up in the Upwork algorithm and attract better work over time. Your first few reviews are not just social proof, they are the infrastructure everything else is built on, so treat the early projects like they matter even when the dollar amounts are small, because the reviews are what actually compound.

Getting started on Upwork without experience comes down to being specific about what you can do, consistent enough to apply through the early rejections, and honest enough that clients trust you with the project.
