How to Get Sober Without Religion or a Higher Power

By Alexis  ·  June 16, 2026  ·  6 min read

The most common complaint people have about AA is the God stuff, and for a significant portion of people who need help, that complaint is enough to keep them from getting it. If you're trying to figure out how to get sober without a religious framework, there are real, evidence-supported options available to you. This article covers what those options are, how they work, and what the research says about their effectiveness.

Why Secular Recovery Is a Legitimate Path

Alcoholics Anonymous was founded in 1935, and its 12-step model has dominated recovery culture in the United States for most of the century since. That cultural dominance can make it feel like the 12 steps are the only real option, but that framing isn't accurate. A 2012 study published in the Journal of Substance Abuse Treatment found that individuals who participated in secular mutual aid groups reported similar improvements in abstinence and quality of life as those in 12-step programs. The mechanism that drives recovery outcomes is peer support and accountability, and that mechanism doesn't require a theological foundation to function.

It's also worth understanding what "higher power" skepticism often actually is. For many people, it isn't blanket anti-spirituality. It's a specific resistance to being asked to surrender personal agency to an external force as a condition of getting well. That's a coherent position, and modern recovery science largely supports the idea that internal locus of control can be a meaningful asset in sustaining sobriety. Cognitive-behavioral approaches, for example, are built around strengthening the individual's capacity to recognize and redirect their own thinking, rather than deferring to anything outside themselves.

None of this is an argument against 12-step programs. They work for a lot of people, and the community they provide is genuinely hard to replicate. The point is that if the spiritual framework is a barrier for you, you have options that are more than compromises or second-best alternatives.

Secular Mutual Aid: SMART Recovery and Refuge Recovery

SMART Recovery (Self-Management and Recovery Training) is probably the most established secular alternative to AA. It's free, widely available in-person and online, and grounded in Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy and cognitive-behavioral techniques. Meetings use a structured format focused on building motivation, managing urges, and solving problems in daily living. SMART has over 3,000 meetings worldwide, including a robust online meeting schedule that makes it accessible regardless of where you live.

Refuge Recovery takes a different angle. It draws on Buddhist philosophy rather than Christian theology, centering mindfulness, meditation, and the concept of suffering as something to be investigated rather than surrendered away from. For people who are open to contemplative practice but resistant to theistic frameworks, it may be a more comfortable fit than either AA or purely cognitive-behavioral approaches. The research base for Refuge Recovery specifically is thinner than for SMART, but the mindfulness practices it draws on have a substantial evidence base in addiction treatment more broadly.

Both of these options function best when treated as communities rather than just meeting formats. Showing up consistently, connecting with people outside of meetings, and finding a level of accountability that resembles having a sponsor in AA, even if it's informal, tends to matter more than which specific program you choose.

Evidence-Based Treatment Options Worth Knowing

Mutual aid groups are valuable, but they're not clinical treatment, and for many people, clinical support is either necessary or significantly helpful at some stage. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) has one of the strongest evidence bases of any intervention for substance use disorders, and it requires no spiritual component whatsoever. It works by helping people identify the thought patterns and situational triggers that drive use, and then practice specific responses to those triggers before they result in relapse.

Medication-Assisted Treatment, or MAT, is another area where secular recovery often has an advantage over purely 12-step culture, which has historically been resistant to medications like naltrexone or buprenorphine. The research on MAT for alcohol and opioid use disorders is extensive. According to SAMHSA, medications for opioid use disorder reduce mortality by approximately 50 percent when used consistently. If a recovery community's ideology discourages you from using a medication that could keep you alive, that's a cost worth weighing carefully.

Working with a therapist who specializes in addiction isn't a replacement for peer community, but it can address dimensions of substance use that mutual aid groups aren't equipped to handle, including underlying trauma, co-occurring mental health conditions, and the specific cognitive distortions that tend to sustain addictive behavior.

Building Structure Without a Spiritual Framework

One underappreciated function of programs like AA is the structure they impose on daily life, especially early sobriety. Meetings give people somewhere to be, something to do with difficult hours, and a reason to leave the house. When you're building a secular recovery path, you may need to be more deliberate about constructing that structure yourself. This might look like committing to a regular exercise schedule, blocking specific hours for therapy or meetings, or building a daily routine that leaves less unstructured time during high-risk periods like evenings.

Accountability doesn't require a sponsor, but it does require another person. Having at least one person who knows your situation and checks in with you regularly is one of the most consistent predictors of sustained sobriety across program types. That person might be a therapist, a trusted friend, a family member who understands addiction, or someone you've connected with through a secular recovery group. The relationship matters more than the label.

Some people find it useful to write down what they're committing to and why, particularly in early recovery. This doesn't need to be a formal step work document. It can be a simple, private list of what life was like, what you want instead, and what specifically you're willing to do to get there. Externalizing the commitment in some form tends to make it more durable than keeping it entirely internal.

What to Realistically Expect in Early Sobriety

The first weeks of sobriety are often physically and psychologically difficult regardless of what path you're on. Sleep may be disrupted for several weeks, particularly if alcohol was involved. Mood may be unstable as brain chemistry recalibrates. Post-Acute Withdrawal Syndrome, or PAWS, can produce intermittent symptoms including anxiety, cognitive fog, and emotional volatility for weeks or months after the acute withdrawal phase ends. Knowing this in advance helps because it means a hard week at month two isn't necessarily a sign that you're failing.

Cravings are normal and, for most people, do diminish in frequency and intensity over time. Research on craving duration suggests that most urges peak and pass within 15 to 30 minutes if not acted on. Techniques like urge surfing, which involves observing a craving with curiosity rather than trying to suppress it, come directly from mindfulness-based approaches and don't require any spiritual belief to use effectively.

Getting sober is harder than staying sober, but staying sober is its own ongoing project. The people who tend to do well long-term are usually the ones who treat their recovery as a system they actively maintain rather than a problem they solved once.

There is no single correct way to get sober, and the absence of religious belief doesn't put you at a disadvantage as long as you're building real structure and real accountability into your life.
